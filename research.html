Research

How can cities delegate consequential decisions to computational systems without losing resilience, accountability or fairness?

Research statement

Cities increasingly delegate consequential decisions to computational systems: how traffic is routed, which bridges and pipes are repaired before they fail, which neighbourhoods receive early warning of a flood, how scarce public services are allocated. Dr Sargiotis's research starts from the conviction that the resilience of urban infrastructure depends on treating the engineering of these systems and the governance of the data they consume as a single problem.

His work proceeds along three connected lines, which correspond to three book series with Springer Nature. AI and the Intelligent City treats urban planning and governance as continuous, data-driven decision processes, from land use and digital twins to the ethics of urban AI. AI and Green Infrastructure addresses mobility, energy, water, waste and construction as systems that can learn and adapt. AI for Human-Centric Cities asks whom intelligent infrastructure serves, examining public policy, health, well-being, inclusion and sustainable civil engineering. Further volumes address data governance, MATLAB for civil engineers, disaster resilience, construction automation, civil engineering and planetary sustainability.

Methodologically, fifteen of the seventeen books share a common form. Most chapters review the published literature, often including documented city initiatives, and then formulate AI frameworks as explicit mathematical objectives and layered architectures, most often combining forecasting models such as LSTM networks and graph neural networks with reinforcement-learning controllers, digital twins and federated, privacy-preserving learning, and ending in a human-in-the-loop layer. In most volumes fairness, privacy and explainability, and in several the energy cost of AI itself, are written into these formulations as constraints rather than added afterwards. Across these fifteen volumes, the result is a sustained programme of conceptual work on how AI could be designed for, and governed by, public institutions.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning

Applied machine learning and computational methods for engineering problems and public-value systems.

This strand concerns the practical deployment of learning-based methods in civil engineering: structural health monitoring, predictive maintenance, optimisation and decision support. The emphasis falls less on novel architectures than on how established techniques, from convolutional and graph neural networks to deep reinforcement learning, can be integrated into engineering workflows whose failure modes carry public consequences.

Data governance

Privacy, transparency, security and accountable information management across public and private organisations.

Intelligent infrastructure is only as trustworthy as the data on which it runs. Work in this area develops governance frameworks, stewardship roles, quality metrics and compliance mechanisms, and examines privacy-preserving techniques such as federated learning and differential privacy as instruments of governance as well as of computation.

Resilient and smart cities

Urban resilience, digital twins, sustainable infrastructure and data-driven city systems.

A substantial part of the recent monograph programme treats the city as an adaptive system that can anticipate and absorb shocks. The books examine digital twins, predictive disaster modelling, climate adaptation, health infrastructure and data-driven urban design, and draw on documented initiatives in cities across Europe, North America, the Middle East and Asia.

Digital transformation

Modernisation of infrastructures, institutions, public services and engineering workflows.

Drawing on more than twenty-five years of running university computing laboratories, at Panteion University in 1998–99 and at NTUA since 2000, this strand addresses digital transformation as an organisational problem as much as a technical one. It includes strategic analysis of Greece's digital transition, e-government interoperability and the automation of construction through robotics, additive manufacturing and building information modelling.

Intelligent transportation systems

Digital transport infrastructure, interoperability and data-enabled mobility.

The most sustained treatment of mobility is AI and the Mobility Revolution, which models urban transport as a set of decision problems: autonomous vehicles, adaptive traffic signals, Hyperloop and high-speed rail, congestion management, electric-vehicle charging, ride-sharing, drones, autonomous freight, smart roads and incident detection. Related volumes address green transport within urban infrastructure and reinforcement learning for traffic control within broader models of urban development, with data privacy, interoperability and scalability treated as preconditions for wider deployment.

Human-centric and ethical AI

Accountability, explainability, inclusion and equitable adoption of intelligent systems.

Empathy in Algorithms and AI with Integrity argue that fairness, transparency and accountability should be specified as design requirements of public-facing AI, and the theme runs through the other volumes as well. This work examines algorithmic bias in policing, transport and public services, formulates fairness-constrained reinforcement learning, adversarial debiasing and participatory forms of explainability, and considers how the digital divide shapes who benefits from smart infrastructure.
